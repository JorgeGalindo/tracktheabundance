Where it comes from

The abundance funding world has a surprisingly precise origin story. In 2006 two Bridgewater Associates analysts, Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld, started a workplace club to find the most cost-effective charities. They quit, founded GiveWell in 2007, and in 2011 partnered with a new foundation — Good Ventures, the vehicle of Facebook/Asana co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and former Wall Street Journal reporter Cari Tuna.

That partnership produced GiveWell Labs (2011), renamed the Open Philanthropy Project in 2014, spun out as an independent LLC in 2017, shortened to Open Philanthropy in 2019, and — on 18 November 2025 — rebranded to Coefficient Giving. The method never changed: "strategic cause selection" by importance, neglectedness and tractability, and "hits-based giving" that accepts many failures for a few outsized wins.

The whole edifice rests on a single anchor donor. Good Ventures holds ~$10 billion and has directed over $4 billion; for years it was essentially the only funder. That dependency is the network's defining strength and its structural fragility — dramatised by the 2024 cause-exits, when Good Ventures quietly stopped funding several sub-causes and the whole ecosystem wobbled.

Running in parallel is a second, wealthier orbit: the Collison brothers and Stripe (valued at $159 billion in February 2026). Patrick Collison launched the Progress Studies movement with Tyler Cowen in 2019, co-founded the Arc Institute and ran the COVID-era Fast Grants. The two orbits have now merged at the funding layer: they co-founded Arc, co-fund the Institute for Progress, and in March 2025 Collison became a named contributor to Coefficient's $120M Abundance & Growth Fund.

Current state · Europe

There is no "European programme" or legal entity — only a London coworking hub and staff dispersed across European time zones. Yet the money is unmistakably arriving, through three channels of very different maturity.

1 · Farm animal welfare — the beachhead that already exists

This is the most concrete, euro-denominated flow. Confirmed grants include €5.5M to Eurogroup for Animals (Brussels), €2.37M to Equalia (Spain), $3M to the Albert Schweitzer Foundation (Germany), plus Compassion in World Farming (UK), Animal Equality (DE/IT/ES/UK) and L214 (France). In March 2026 four of these took the European Commission to the Court of Justice of the EU over its failure to deliver the "End the Cage Age" pledge — grantee money translating directly into EU litigation.

2 · AI & biosecurity governance — the policy pipeline

The largest single recent grant is $25.6M to BlueDot Impact (UK) for AI-safety workforce-building; the Centre for Long-Term Resilience (~£4M + match) engaged the EU Biotech Act consultation. The Talos Network in Brussels — historically majority-funded by Coefficient/Good Ventures — places fellows inside the EU AI Office, OECD.AI and The Future Society. When the EU's GPAI Code of Practice was weakened in 2025, it was these funded organisations that publicly objected.

3 · Abundance / progress — the newest, most political channel

Collison-spearheaded Progress Ireland saw its "garden homes" idea adopted by the Irish government, and John Collison's October 2025 Irish Times essay on planning sclerosis became a genuine political event. Stripe-owned Works in Progress anchors the UK YIMBY node. But the Abundance & Growth Fund itself remains "primarily in the United States" — a grant to a group like Britain Remade would be the tell that it is internationalising.

The public grants database is being deprecated post-rebrand, and Coefficient says European grantees "benefit from having identifiably European funders" — so it increasingly advises European donors rather than granting centrally. The upshot: European activity is systematically under-represented in what you can see publicly. The map on this site is a floor, not a ceiling.

Tensions & critique

  • Billionaire capture. The American Prospect's June 2026 reporting on an "Abundance Capital Stack" frames the movement as a nine-figure, Silicon-Valley-funded deregulation project; Jacobin and others contest whether abundance is genuine state-capacity reform or "neoliberalism repackaged."
  • Conflict of interest. The Karnofsky–Amodei marriage, Coefficient alumni across CSET/RAND/NIST/OpenAI, and the Talos fellowship-to-EU-institution pipeline all raise the question of funders shaping the rules for an industry they are invested in.
  • Single-donor fragility. The 2024 cause-exits showed how much of the field depends on one household's judgement — only partly mitigated by the Navigation Fund and the multi-donor pivot.
  • Contested foundations. The "science is slowing" thesis underpinning Progress Studies is academically disputed; even Collison has conceded the effect may be modest.

What to watch

  • A European pooled fund or a Brussels/London programme (not just a coworking desk) — the signal that Europe becomes a first-class theatre.
  • A first European abundance/YIMBY grant (e.g. Britain Remade) — proof the Abundance & Growth Fund is internationalising.
  • The EU Biotech Act and AI Act implementation — where funded biosecurity/AI groups most directly touch legislation.
  • The Anthropic IPO — potentially the largest concentration of EA-aligned philanthropic capital in history, and the biggest possible shifter of everything above.
