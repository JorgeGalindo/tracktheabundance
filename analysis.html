Where this all comes
from — and who is who.

One funding network, built over two decades, is now active in Europe. Here is the lineage from a 2006 charity-evaluation project to a grantmaker directing over $1B a year, the people who recur across philanthropy, frontier AI and government, and the documented state of play on the continent today.

Where it comes from

The abundance funding world has a well-documented origin. In 2006 two Bridgewater Associates analysts, Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld, started a workplace club to find the most cost-effective charities. They quit, founded GiveWell in 2007, and in 2011 partnered with a new foundation — Good Ventures, the vehicle of Facebook/Asana co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and former Wall Street Journal reporter Cari Tuna.

That partnership produced GiveWell Labs (2011), renamed the Open Philanthropy Project in 2014, spun out as an independent LLC in 2017, shortened to Open Philanthropy in 2019, and — on 18 November 2025 — rebranded to Coefficient Giving. The method never changed: "strategic cause selection" by importance, neglectedness and tractability, and "hits-based giving" that accepts many failures for a few outsized wins.

The structure rests on a single anchor donor. Good Ventures holds ~$10 billion and has directed over $4 billion; for years it was essentially the only funder. That dependency is both the network's main strength and its main structural risk — visible in the 2024 cause-exits, when Good Ventures stopped funding several sub-causes and multiple organisations lost their primary funder at once.

Running in parallel is a second orbit: the Collison brothers and Stripe (valued at $159 billion in February 2026). Patrick Collison launched the Progress Studies movement with Tyler Cowen in 2019, co-founded the Arc Institute and ran the COVID-era Fast Grants. The two orbits now co-fund at several documented points: both are founding donors of Arc, both fund the Institute for Progress, and in March 2025 Collison became a named contributor to Coefficient's $120M Abundance & Growth Fund.

Current state · Europe

There is no "European programme" or legal entity — only a London coworking hub and staff dispersed across European time zones. Confirmed grants are nevertheless arriving, through four channels of very different maturity.

1 · Farm animal welfare — the most established channel

This is the largest confirmed, euro-denominated flow. Grants include €5.5M to Eurogroup for Animals (Brussels), €2.37M to Equalia (Spain), $3M to the Albert Schweitzer Foundation (Germany), plus Compassion in World Farming (UK), Animal Equality (DE/IT/ES/UK) and L214 (France) — mostly multi-year general support. In March 2026 four of these organisations took the European Commission to the Court of Justice of the EU over its failure to deliver the "End the Cage Age" pledge — funded organisations pursuing litigation at EU level.

2 · AI & biosecurity governance — the policy channel

The largest single recent grant is $25.6M to BlueDot Impact (UK) for AI-safety workforce-building; the Centre for Long-Term Resilience (~£4M, plus a pledge to match up to £3M from other donors 1:1) engaged the EU Biotech Act consultation. The Talos Network in Brussels — historically majority-funded by Coefficient/Good Ventures — places fellows at the EU AI Office, OECD.AI and The Future Society. When the final GPAI Code of Practice dropped several provisions in 2025, these funded organisations publicly objected.

3 · Abundance / progress — the newest channel

Collison-backed Progress Ireland saw its "garden homes" proposal adopted by the Irish government, and John Collison's October 2025 Irish Times essay on planning reform drew wide political attention. Stripe-owned Works in Progress anchors the UK YIMBY node. But the Abundance & Growth Fund itself remains "primarily in the United States" — a grant to a group like Britain Remade would be the first evidence of internationalisation.

4 · Effective giving — the regranting layer

Coefficient's effective-giving RFP funds European donation platforms: Doneer Effectief (Netherlands; over $9M raised in 2025, up from ~€6.3M in 2024) and Effektiv Spenden (Germany). These platforms regrant onward — Effektiv Spenden's AI-safety funds sent €170,227 to SaferAI in H1 2025 — so network-aligned money reaches European policy organisations one step removed from any Coefficient grant record. Coefficient's mid-2025 Global Growth Fund sits alongside these but targets low- and middle-income economies.

The public grants database is being deprecated post-rebrand, and Coefficient says European grantees "benefit from having identifiably European funders" — so it increasingly advises European donors rather than granting centrally. The upshot: European activity is systematically under-represented in what you can see publicly. The map on this site is a floor, not a ceiling.

How grants are made

Who gets funded next is largely determined by which door the money uses. Five mechanisms account for every confirmed European grant on this site.

  • Direct multi-year general support. The dominant form in farm animal welfare and for the largest AI grants (BlueDot's $25.6M is three-year general support). Grantees are typically organisations with an existing track record in a Coefficient cause area; grants run roughly €2–6M over 2–3 years at the European scale.
  • Open RFPs and EOI cycles. The scheduled route in: the AI Governance and Technical AI Safety RFPs are explicitly open to European applicants, the Biosecurity fund runs expression-of-interest rounds (the last closed 11 May 2026), and the effective-giving RFP is how Doneer Effectief and Effektiv Spenden entered the portfolio. New cycles are the most likely origin of the next European grantees.
  • Matching pledges. CLTR's deal — every £1 from other donors matched 1:1 up to £3M — shows Coefficient using its money to recruit other funders for a European organisation rather than remaining sole funder.
  • Regranting through funded platforms. Effektiv Spenden's €170k to SaferAI is Coefficient-ecosystem money arriving via a European intermediary, invisible in any central database.
  • Advised European donors. Coefficient says European policy grantees "benefit from having identifiably European funders" and therefore advises European donors rather than granting centrally — the largest and least visible mechanism, and the reason the public record understates activity.

Around the core sit adjacent funders sharing grantees — the Navigation Fund (which entered causes Good Ventures exited), Jaan Tallinn's Survival & Flourishing Fund, and Tyler Cowen's Emergent Ventures (small fast grants to individuals, including Europeans) — so an organisation legible to one of these funders tends to become legible to the others.

Tensions & critique

  • Donor concentration and influence. The American Prospect's June 2026 reporting on an "Abundance Capital Stack" describes pledges of up to $260M/year from tech donors (figures from a leaked fundraising memo; organisers say actual grants are closer to $40M/year). Jacobin and others debate whether abundance is state-capacity reform or "neoliberalism repackaged."
  • Conflict of interest. The Karnofsky–Amodei marriage, Coefficient alumni across CSET/RAND/NIST/OpenAI, and the Talos fellowship-to-EU-institution pipeline all raise the question of funders shaping the rules for an industry they are invested in.
  • Single-donor fragility. The 2024 cause-exits showed how much of the field depends on one household's judgement — only partly mitigated by the Navigation Fund and the multi-donor pivot.
  • Contested foundations. The "science is slowing" thesis underpinning Progress Studies is academically disputed; even Collison has conceded the effect may be modest.

What to watch

  • New RFP and EOI cycles from the Navigating Transformative AI, Biosecurity and effective-giving funds — the scheduled mechanism by which new European organisations enter the portfolio.
  • A European pooled fund or a Brussels/London programme (not just a coworking desk) — the signal that Europe becomes a first-class theatre. Other thresholds: a European legal entity, or a Europe-specific programme-lead hire.
  • A first European abundance/YIMBY grant (e.g. Britain Remade) — evidence the Abundance & Growth Fund is internationalising.
  • The EU Biotech Act and AI Act implementation — where funded biosecurity/AI groups most directly touch legislation, and where policy-analysis capacity is what gets funded.
  • The Anthropic IPO — a potential new concentration of EA-aligned philanthropic capital, and the largest possible shifter of everything above.
